Two books, one crack in the price.

Arbitrage isn't a hot take on who wins. It's a coverage problem: buy every possible outcome for less than the guaranteed payout, and the winner stops being your problem. The math is exact. Getting both legs filled before the crack closes is not.

SO / ARB–017 minute readUpdated Aug 2026

Every price is a probability wearing a disguise. Translate two venues' odds into the same units and they usually agree closely enough that nothing's there. Occasionally they don't — and that gap, however small, is real money sitting in plain sight until someone closes it.

01

Turn odds into probability

Decimal odds O imply a raw probability of 1/O. A normal bookmaker market sums above 100%; that excess is the house's cut, not a forecast.

Pᵢ = 1 / Oᵢ · overround = Σ(1 / Oᵢ) − 1

Odds of 2.15 imply 46.5%. A tight two-way tennis market might total 104% at one book — a 4-point cut. A ten-team futures board can run 130%+ without anyone blinking. Arbitrage isn't about spotting the cut. It's about finding the rare case where the best price for every outcome, shopped across separate books, totals under 100% anyway.

ODDS IN ANY FORMATFractional (6/4) and American (+150 / −150) prices convert to the same decimal number before any of this math applies: fractional → decimal is (numerator/denominator) + 1, and American → decimal is (odds/100) + 1 for positive lines or (100/|odds|) + 1 for negative lines. 6/4 and +150 are both 2.50; −150 is 1.667. ShortOpen normalizes every quote to decimal first so the probability math never depends on which format a book happens to display.
Overround by market type

Each bar is the summed implied probability across a market's outcomes. Two of these clear 100% easily — normal bookmaker cut. The shopped best-of-books line is the only one that can fall under 100%, and only occasionally.

The gap between "one book's overround" and "the shopped-best overround" is the entire trade. Pull the single best price for each outcome from a pool of books — one might lead on the favorite, another on the underdog — and sum those best-of prices instead of any one book's full board. Most of the time that shopped sum still clears 100%, just by a smaller margin than any individual book. Occasionally, when two books disagree enough about where the true number sits, it doesn't.

02

The negative-hold test

A = Σ(1 / best Oᵢ) · arbitrage exists when A < 1
SHORTOPEN RULEEvery settlement state has to be covered — no exceptions. Two attractive prices pulled from a five-outcome market aren't an arbitrage; they're two directional bets that happen to look cheap on the same day. If the field can still produce a result you haven't priced, you don't have a lock.
The negative-hold line

A is the sum of implied probabilities at the best shopped price for every leg. Left of the 1.00 line, the venues are collectively pricing the field at less than certainty — that gap is the trade.

A is just the sum of implied probabilities across every leg you'd need to hold, each priced at its best available quote across books. Below 1.00, the market is — collectively, across venues — pricing the field at less than certainty, which means someone is willing to pay you more than fair value for at least one outcome. A two-way market (moneyline, over/under) needs two legs covered; a three-way soccer match (home/draw/away) needs all three; a golf outright field needs every player with any realistic chance. Miss a leg — say, skip the draw in a soccer match because it looks unattractive — and A < 1 across the two prices you did check tells you nothing, because the draw can still happen and you've priced zero of it.

03

Equalize the payout

Finding A < 1 only proves the free lunch exists. Getting paid the same amount no matter who wins is a separate allocation problem, solved by weighting each stake to its own implied probability.

Sᵢ = Sₜₒₜₐₗ × [(1/Oᵢ) / A] R = Sₜₒₜₐₗ / A ROI = (1/A − 1) × 100%

04

The worked lock

1

Book A prices Side A at 2.15 (46.5%). Book B prices Side B at 2.05 (48.8%). Combined: 95.3% — under 100%, so the lock is real.

2

A $1,000 pool splits to roughly $488 on Side A and $512 on Side B, weighted by each leg's share of that 95.3%.

3

Either result pays back about $1,049 — a locked 4.9% before costs, execution slippage, and whatever it takes to actually get both bets down.

4

That 4.9% is R/Stotal − 1, where R = Stotal / A is the guaranteed payout regardless of outcome. It is not annualized — if the market settles in three days, the return over a full year, compounded and repeated at the same rate, would be dramatically higher than the raw 4.9% figure suggests, but that assumes you can find and size a comparable lock every three days, which real markets rarely offer on demand.

05

Where perfect math breaks

A few seconds of latency between legs turns a locked position into a directional bet you didn't mean to take. A tennis retirement rule that pays differently at two books breaks the equivalence the whole calculation assumed. Bet limits, rejected orders, rounding, and thin depth all eat into a number that looked clean on a spreadsheet.

EXECUTION FILTERShortOpen calls a lock only after four things check out: matching contract language across both books, real executable depth at the quoted price, all-in costs including fees, and a fill sequence that doesn't leave you exposed mid-trade. A spreadsheet profit isn't a tradable profit until all four clear.

Depth matters as much as price. A book showing 2.15 on a $50 limit isn't the same trade as 2.15 on a $5,000 limit — the arbitrage calculation assumes you can actually get your full stake matched at the quoted number, and thin order books quietly reprice against you the moment you try. Sequencing compounds the problem: placing the larger leg first, before confirming the smaller leg is still available at its quoted price, can leave one side filled and the other gone, converting a locked position into a naked directional bet in the seconds between clicks.

06

The target is the all-in price

ShortOpen doesn't publish "arbitrage found" and stop there. It publishes a ceiling — the most the whole basket is worth paying once fees, slippage, and a safety margin are already subtracted. Cross that ceiling and the edge is gone, no matter what the raw pre-cost math still claims.

SO max basket = $1.00 − fees − slippage − execution reserve
Where the dollar goes

Fees, slippage and execution reserve are subtracted from a dollar first — what's left on the green segment is the actual ceiling ShortOpen will pay for the whole basket.

Each term is a specific, estimable cost rather than a vague haircut. Fees are whatever the venue actually charges to place and settle the trade — a flat commission, a spread markup, or a payment-processing cut. Slippage is the gap ShortOpen expects between the quoted price and the price actually filled, sized to that market's typical depth and volatility. Execution reserve is a deliberate safety margin held back for the unpriced risk in section 05 — timing gaps, contract mismatches, rejected orders — sized larger for markets with thin books or unclear settlement rules, and smaller for liquid, well-defined ones. Subtract all three from a dollar and what's left is the most the whole basket can cost and still be worth buying.
