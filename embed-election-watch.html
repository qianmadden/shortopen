How we're building this

We started this desk the same way we started the sports side: reading a lot, and noticing the same question every cycle. Race-rating outlets — Cook Political Report, Ballotpedia, Sabato's Crystal Ball — publish labels like Likely, Lean, Tilt, Toss-up. We converted those labels onto one 0–100 scale, sourced and cited on every card, so races that started from different words can actually sit side by side. That's the Consensus Read — not our opinion, just a common ruler applied to what the outlets already said.

The ShortOpen Read is us doing our own homework on top of that: a district's voting history across several cycles instead of just the last one, how close the incumbent actually won last time, and the fundraising gap between the two campaigns as of the most recent filing. We also hold recent rating changes at half-weight for a stretch — a shift made in the last 60 days need a second independent signal, a new poll or filing or another outlet moving too, before we treat it as real rather than noise.

We're not pollsters, and we don't have decades of election-forecasting pedigree behind us. What we do have is a journalism background, and we're putting it to use here the same way we use it on the sports and markets side: chase the primary sources, read past the headline label, and say plainly where our own number differs from the consensus and why. This desk grew out of the same instinct that built the rest of ShortOpen — we like markets, we like odds, and election forecasting is just another public number worth checking against the underlying facts. Most of the time our number lands close to consensus, which is exactly what should happen when a race is well-covered. When it doesn't, we flag the gap on the card and show our reasoning, so you can judge whether we're onto something or just wrong. Prediction markets have become a hot commodity, and we know it. We're not going to pretend we can source every data point in real time or hand you a fresh number every thirty seconds — that's not what a small research desk does well, and we'd rather be straight about that than fake a live feed we don't have. What we can do, as a group of trained journalists, is chase the primary sources, read past the headline, and give you an honest lean on where a race is actually heading — not just where the latest push notification says it stands.
