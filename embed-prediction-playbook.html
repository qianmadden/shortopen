ShortOpen Research / Field Guide

The crowd is a price. Not a source.

A market pricing something at 64% isn't a scientific measurement of the future. It's where the last trade cleared, under one rule set, at one moment, with whatever liquidity happened to show up. Winning here isn't about trusting that number — it's about being able to explain it.

SO / PM–029 minute readUpdated Aug 2026

A 64% price doesn't mean the event has a measured 64% chance of happening. It means that's where supply and demand last agreed to disagree. Your job is not to trust the number. Your job is to explain it.

01

Start with a prior, not the ticker

Before opening the market, build a reference-class estimate: team strength, polling base rates, incumbency history, an institutional timeline, or another defensible baseline that has nothing to do with today's price.

posterior odds = prior odds × evidence likelihood ratio

The market can update your prior. It should never become your prior. Anchor to the current number first and every later move just rewrites your conviction back at you without adding a single fact.

02

Ask who could know — and how it would show

Most venues never reveal who's trading. Don't invent a "smart money" story to fill that gap. Use behavioral proxies instead: size that survives a move against it, orders that keep reloading instead of vanishing, and repricing that lines up with a verifiable news timestamp rather than a rumor.

FLOW IS A CLUE, NOT PROOFOne large print can be a hedge, a forced rebalance, or a publicity trade — not inside information. Treat it as one weak signal, credible only when price, size, timing, and outside evidence all point the same direction at once.
03

Volume is activity; liquidity is permission

A market can show heavy lifetime volume and still be nearly impossible to move real size in right now. Headline volume counts every trade that's ever happened. What matters at entry is what will fill today.

effective edge = fair probability − entry price − fees − slippage − resolution risk
V

Volume: how much has traded over the market's whole life — attention, not tradability.

D

Depth: how much can trade right now without moving the price against you.

S

Spread: the immediate cost of crossing from the best bid to the best ask.

04

Separate news from narrative

Fresh, resolution-relevant information deserves weight. Viral attention does not. Keep a timestamped ledger: what actually changed, whether it touches the exact resolution criteria, and how much of the price move happened before the evidence went public versus after.

REACTION TESTIf the price moves without a real change in the event's underlying inputs, demand a bigger discount before acting on it. If verified evidence does shift the base rate, update the probability — not just the story you're telling yourself about it.
05

Read the rules before the crowd

Resolution source, date boundaries, recounts, substitutions, postponements, and ambiguous wording can decide the outcome more than the real-world event itself. A correct real-world call can still lose under a different technical definition of what settles it.

If you can't state, in one sentence, the exact document, score, or announcement that settles the outcome, you don't yet understand what you're looking at.

06

Build a probability band, not a point guess

A single-point forecast hides exactly how uncertain it really is. ShortOpen publishes a range instead: the low case reflects missing or adverse evidence, the high case reflects the strongest defensible read of what's known.

SO fair-chance band = model probability ± uncertainty SO good-buy ceiling = lower fair bound − required edge

Entry only makes sense below the lower edge of that range. That gap is your margin of safety — if the price rises above the ceiling, the same event can move from a genuine buy to a pass without a single fact about it changing.

07

The ShortOpen decision stack

1

Define: rewrite the resolution rule in one sentence before doing anything else.

2

Anchor: set an outside prior before you've read the current price.

3

Inspect: weigh volume against real, current depth and spread — not history.

4

Attribute: tie every price move to timestamped evidence; skip identity stories you can't verify.

5

Price: publish a fair-chance band and a good-buy ceiling, not one number.

6

Exit: decide in advance what price or evidence kills the position.

08

How to come out on top

The durable edge is procedural, not psychic. Bet less often. Demand a bigger discount in thin or emotionally charged markets. Size positions to the width of your uncertainty band, not to how confident you feel. Keep a forecast log that records the probability before the result is known — the only honest way to grade yourself. Judge the process by calibration across dozens of calls, not by one dramatic win you'll remember and forty quiet losses you won't.

THE SHORTOPEN CALLDon't just ask "will this happen?" Ask "what probability range is defensible here, what price actually pays me for being wrong, and what specific evidence would change my mind?" If you can't answer all three, you're not ready to act on the number — you're just reacting to it.
